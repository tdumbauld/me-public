What the options are charging for an earnings print, how much of that is the jump itself, and what a straddle is really worth on the morning you close it.

Inputs

? Two expiries, because one cannot do it. An option price near earnings holds two things: the scheduled jump, and the ordinary drift of the stock over the days around it. A single straddle gives you their total and no way to split it.

The front expiry contains the event. The back contains the same drift over a longer life and the same jump, because a jump fixed to a date does not grow with time. Two equations, two unknowns.

Keep the back expiry inside the quarter. Past the next earnings date it holds two jumps and this prices them as one.

The strike is taken as spot and the front straddle is treated as your fill. A straddle struck away from spot does not invert correctly, and a worked fill is worse than the mid you typed.

Event

? The implied move is not the event. It is the jump and the drift combined, and they combine by variance rather than addition, so they do not sum to it.

Crush is not profit. It accrues to whoever is short premium whatever the stock does, so it is a mechanic rather than a strategy, and it is already inside the price you paid or received.

The event figure is sturdy and the crush is fragile, which reads backwards. A small error in the back straddle moves post-event vol a long way and the event estimate barely at all, because the event term absorbs the residual. The sensitivity line below is computed from your own inputs for that reason.

Known bias: the crush here runs 10% to 30% too big. The drift rate is measured between your two expiries, then used as the level the front one collapses to. The front's remaining life is the stretch right after the print, which runs hotter: across 1,854 events the five sessions after an announcement ran about 1.10x the twenty after. The closer the back expiry, the worse it bites.

Straddle

? The break even is the whole premium, not the event. A straddle pays only once the move clears everything you paid, and what you paid covers the jump AND the drift around it.

An ATM straddle always breaks even near 0.80 standard deviations. That falls out of the arithmetic rather than the market: a straddle costs about sqrt(2/pi) = 0.798 of one standard deviation whatever the name. So the chance of clearing it sits near 42% every time, and a long straddle held to expiration is roughly a 42% proposition before costs.

A short straddle has no defined maximum loss and this reports none rather than a large number, because a figure there invites sizing it like a defined-risk trade. The broker's margin is not a maximum loss.

No spread and no commission. You typed a mid and this believes you. A census across ten names found two-leg structures quoted at a median of 12% of premium.

Payoff diagram

? Three curves, and the gaps between them are the content. Grey is the position at expiration, which is what every platform draws. White is the same position at your exit, with the crush applied and the remaining days still in it: white because it is the mark this trade is actually closed at. The dashed line is the same exit with no crush at all.

The dashed line is the mistake made visible. It is what your platform's theta tells you, holding implied vol flat. The distance between it and the white curve is the crush, and on an earnings position it is not a rounding error.

They read by lightness, not hue, so no curve reads as a verdict and all three survive a colour-blind reader or a greyscale print. That convention comes from the terminal these are drawn like.

The shaded band is the event move backed out of your two quotes: what the market has priced the announcement at. Not a forecast and not a confidence interval.

The curves must converge in the tails. Far from the strike no extrinsic remains, so all three meet. That is a structural check on the arithmetic, not a drawing choice.
